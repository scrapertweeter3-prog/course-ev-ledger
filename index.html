The course costs $2,751 before it teaches anything

A $997 course that promises to beat the market looks cheap next to the $100,000 it claims to manage. Price the money instead of the product. $997 parked in an index fund at 7 percent for the same 15 years grows to $2,751, and that forgone compounding is what buying the course actually costs. The receipt was never the sticker.

The promise prices the other side. The pitch amounts to a point of extra return, so price the point. $100,000 at 7 percent for 15 years is $275,903. At 8 percent it is $317,217. One point of return over 15 years on $100,000 is $41,314, and that is the ceiling on what the course can hand you even if it performs exactly as advertised.

Now the ask against the prize. The course price is 2.4 percent of the prize it promises, which reads like a bargain until the advice is priced as a bet. Give it a 1-in-10 chance of ever changing how you actually invest, and a changed investor captures the full point, so expected value is $4,131 against a $2,751 real cost. The receipt-only version of the math says buy. The version that charges the money its index job leaves a margin of $1,380 on a bet whose commonest outcome is modules that go unwatched and pay zero.

The decision rule survives every pitch: never judge a course by its price, judge it by the point of return it claims times the chance you do anything about it. Expected-value math like this is the standing beat at Firenomics, same ledger at firenomics.com.

Nobody compounds the receipt.
